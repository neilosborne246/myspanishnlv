Buying a House in Spain: Everything Expats Need to Know in 2026

A frank guide to the practical experience of buying Spanish property — regional realities, hidden costs, and what can go wrong at each stage.

The Reality Behind the Dream: What Expat Buyers Actually Experience

Glossy property websites and lifestyle magazines make buying a house in Spain look effortless — a sun-drenched afternoon with the estate agent, a handshake over tapas, and a set of keys by the weekend. The reality is considerably more nuanced. Spain's property market is genuine and attractive, but it operates under its own rules, with its own timelines, its own professional actors, and its own particular risks that differ substantially from what buyers from the UK, US, Australia, or Canada might expect at home.

This guide is written for expats who want a frank, practical account of what the experience of buying a house in Spain actually involves — not a promotional version, but the real picture, including the parts that can go wrong if you are not prepared. Combined with good legal advice, understanding what to expect is your most powerful tool.

Regional Differences That Matter

Spain is not a single property market. It is seventeen autonomous communities with their own property transfer tax rates, their own local planning rules, their own cultures around price negotiation, and radically different stock, pace, and buyer demographics. Where you choose to buy will shape every aspect of your experience.

Costa del Sol — the mature market

The Costa del Sol, stretching from Nerja in the east through Málaga, Marbella, Estepona, and Sotogrande, is the most internationally mature property market in Spain. The infrastructure for foreign buyers — bilingual agents, English-speaking lawyers, international schools, private hospitals — is well established. Supply is tighter than a decade ago and prices have appreciated strongly. Marbella's Golden Mile and Benahavís are genuinely luxury markets; further along the coast, areas like Fuengirola and Torre del Mar offer genuine value by comparison. Expect competition for good properties and negotiating room of 3–8% below asking price on well-priced listings.

The quirks of this market include a high proportion of non-resident vendors (creating specific tax withholding obligations for buyers — see below) and, in rural parts of the province, a significant legacy of unlicensed building from the 1980s and 1990s boom. Your lawyer's planning checks are non-negotiable here.

Costa Blanca — value and accessibility

The Costa Blanca — Alicante, Torrevieja, Altea, Benidorm, Denia, Jávea — attracts buyers who want Mediterranean living at more accessible prices than the Costa del Sol. The northern Costa Blanca (Altea, Jávea, Dénia) is more expensive and quieter than the southern end (Torrevieja, Orihuela Costa), which offers the most affordable coastal property in mainland Spain. Both have large, well-established British, Irish, Scandinavian, and German expat communities and a solid support infrastructure. Property transfer tax in the Valencia region has recently shifted — check the current ITP rate with your lawyer at time of purchase.

The Balearic Islands — premium living

Mallorca, Menorca, and Ibiza operate as separate, premium-priced markets. Palma de Mallorca has become a genuinely cosmopolitan city with a sophisticated dining, arts, and business culture. Prime property in Mallorca (south-west coast, around Santa Ponsa, Calvià, Andratx) commands prices comparable with the best parts of the Côte d'Azur. The islands have specific purchase restrictions for certain property types — your lawyer must check Balearic Land Law compliance — and rental licence regulations are among the strictest in Spain.

Interior Spain — value and character

For buyers prioritising space, character, and budget over beach access, inland Spain — Extremadura, Castile-La Mancha, parts of Murcia, inland Andalusia — offers extraordinary value. A substantial rural finca with land and outbuildings can cost under €200,000 in some areas. The due diligence challenges are specific and significant: rural properties are more likely to have unlicensed building works, informal utility connections, and complex cadastral histories. Many NLV applicants who choose inland Spain find that the cost savings on property allow them to meet the NLV income threshold more comfortably.

New Build vs. Resale: Which Is Right for You?

The choice between a new build (obra nueva) and a resale property (segunda mano or vivienda de segunda mano) involves genuine trade-offs on both sides.

New build advantages and risks

A new build comes with a ten-year structural guarantee under Spanish law, modern energy efficiency ratings, contemporary layouts, and — in theory — no immediate maintenance requirements. For buyers who value certainty of specification and modern amenities, new builds are attractive. The tax structure differs: you pay 10% IVA (VAT) plus AJD stamp duty rather than the resale ITP.

The risks of new build are specific and serious. Off-plan purchases — buying before or during construction — require that the developer holds bank guarantees for all stage payments. Without these guarantees, if the developer encounters financial difficulty, your money may be at risk. Spain has a painful history of buyers losing deposits and stage payments when developers went into administration during the 2008–2013 downturn. Reputable developers now routinely provide proper guarantees, but your lawyer must verify this for every payment.

New builds also carry the risk of delivery delays and specification changes. Always ensure your purchase contract includes specific penalty clauses for late completion and defines the specification precisely with reference to detailed plans and finishes schedules.

Resale advantages and risks

A resale property is what it says it is — you can see exactly what you are buying, assess the condition, check the neighbours, verify that the pool actually works, and understand the community. The buying timeline is faster (typically two to three months from offer to completion versus six to eighteen months for off-plan), and you can move in immediately after completion.

The specific risks of resale centre on what the previous owner may have done — or not done. Unlicensed extensions, uncleared mortgages, unpaid community fees, and outstanding IBI arrears are the classic issues. All of these are discoverable through proper due diligence, which is why your lawyer's investigation is so important.

The Arras Contract: Your Largest Pre-Completion Commitment

The contrato de arras deserves its own section because it is the moment at which real money is committed and real legal obligations are created. Understanding this contract before you sign it can save you from significant financial loss.

At the arras stage, you typically pay 10% of the agreed purchase price. This payment is simultaneously evidence of your seriousness and a legal commitment. The bilateral penalties built into the arras under Article 1454 of the Spanish Civil Code are significant: if you withdraw without just cause, you forfeit the entire 10%. If the seller withdraws, they must pay you back double — your 10% plus an equivalent penalty. This structure creates a powerful mutual commitment, which is its purpose, but it also means that signing the arras without adequate legal review of the property and the contract is a high-stakes gamble.

Your lawyer should negotiate the following protective clauses before you sign:

  • A condition making the contract subject to satisfactory planning checks and land registry confirmation of no debts
  • A condition allowing withdrawal if a structural survey reveals defects above a defined cost threshold
  • A mortgage condition clause if you are financing the purchase — allowing withdrawal if your mortgage application is declined or approved on materially worse terms than anticipated
  • A precise completion date with defined consequences for delay
  • Confirmation that all chattels, fixtures, and fittings to be included are listed

Cash Purchase vs. Mortgage: The Practical Realities

A significant proportion of international buyers in Spain purchase with cash, particularly in the over-€500,000 market. Cash purchases offer speed — a cash buyer can complete within a month of signing the arras if due diligence is clean — and they remove the risk of the transaction collapsing due to mortgage complications. For vendors, a cash buyer is genuinely preferred and may justify a small price reduction.

For buyers who need mortgage finance, Spain's banking system is accessible to foreign buyers but operates on different terms to what residents experience. Non-residents are typically limited to 60–70% loan-to-value, meaning you need 30–40% as a deposit before taxes and fees. Once you hold NLV and TIE (residence card), Spanish banks reclassify you as a resident and may offer up to 80% LTV at better interest rates. This is one of the practical reasons why coordinating your property purchase with your NLV application makes financial sense.

Hidden Costs That Catch Buyers by Surprise

Beyond the 10–12% on top of the purchase price that we always recommend budgeting for taxes and fees, there are additional costs that frequently catch unprepared buyers:

  • The 3% non-resident vendor withholding: If the seller of the property is a non-resident in Spain, you as the buyer are legally obligated to withhold 3% of the purchase price and pay it directly to the Spanish tax authority on the seller's behalf. This is not a tax you pay on top of the price — it is deducted from the amount you pay the seller. But if your lawyer fails to execute this correctly, you can be held liable for the seller's unpaid capital gains tax against the property.
  • Community fees in arrears: If the community of owners is owed money by the previous owner, that debt transfers to you. Always request a certificate of no outstanding fees as part of pre-completion due diligence.
  • Ongoing annual costs: IBI (council tax, typically 0.3–1.1% of cadastral value), buildings insurance, community maintenance fees (€500–€3,000+ per year depending on development), and non-resident income tax if you are not a Spanish tax resident.
  • Currency conversion: If your purchase funds are in GBP, USD, AUD, or any currency other than euros, exchange rate movements between the time you agree the price and the date of completion can significantly affect the cost in your home currency. Consider using a currency specialist or forward contract to lock in your rate.

The Timeline: What to Expect From Start to Completion

A typical resale property purchase in Spain takes two to three months from the moment your offer is accepted to completion at the notary. The timeline typically looks like this:

  • Week 1–2: Offer accepted; reservation deposit paid (€3,000–€6,000); lawyer instructed; NIE application begins if not already held
  • Week 2–4: Due diligence — Nota Simple, planning checks, community certificate, IBI verification; structural survey if desired
  • Week 3–5: Arras contract drafted, negotiated, and signed; 10% deposit paid
  • Week 6–8: Mortgage application processed (if applicable); any outstanding issues resolved
  • Week 8–12: Completion at notary; balance paid; keys received
  • Week 12–14: Taxes paid within 30-day window; Land Registry inscription filed; utilities transferred

Cash purchases can be faster — sometimes completing within four to six weeks of offer — while mortgage-dependent or complex transactions may take four months or more. Off-plan purchases have an entirely different and longer timeline, often twelve to twenty-four months from exchange to delivery.

What Can Go Wrong — and How to Avoid It

Understanding the most common points of failure is as important as understanding the process itself.

  • Illegal building works discovered late: Without proper planning checks before the arras, you may sign a binding contract for a property with an unlicensed extension. Avoid by instructing your lawyer before paying any deposit.
  • The seller accepts a higher offer after you have paid the reservation: This does happen. The reservation agreement protects you — the seller must return your reservation deposit in full if they sell to someone else, though recovering your money requires legal action. The arras contract offers much stronger protection.
  • Mortgage complications: Banks can change their lending criteria, take longer than expected, or require additional documentation. Always include a mortgage contingency clause in your arras contract.
  • Currency risk: A significant depreciation in your home currency between offer and completion can substantially increase the effective cost of the purchase. Forward contracts eliminate this risk.
  • Community disputes: Some communities of owners carry ongoing disputes about maintenance liability, special levies, or building works. Your lawyer should review the minutes of recent community meetings as part of due diligence.
