Buying Property in Spain as a Foreigner: The Complete Guide

Everything you need to know about the purchase process, true costs, and how your Non-Lucrative Visa fits into the picture.

Can Foreigners Buy Property in Spain?

The short answer is yes — and the rules are refreshingly straightforward. Spain places no restrictions on property ownership by foreign nationals, whether you hold an EU passport or come from the United States, Canada, Australia, the UK, or anywhere else in the world. You have exactly the same ownership rights as a Spanish citizen once the purchase is completed and registered in your name at the Land Registry (Registro de la Propiedad).

This openness is one of the reasons Spain consistently ranks among Europe's top destinations for international property buyers. In recent years, foreign nationals have accounted for roughly 15% of all residential property transactions in Spain — a figure that has held firm even as broader market conditions have shifted.

There is one important legal myth to dispel before we go any further: buying a property in Spain does not automatically grant you the right to live there. Non-EU buyers who wish to spend more than 90 days in any 180-day period must hold a valid visa or residence permit. For many buyers, the Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the natural companion to a Spanish property purchase — it allows you to live legally in Spain on passive income without working locally. We will return to this connection throughout the guide.

Step 1 — Get Your NIE Number Before Anything Else

The Número de Identidad de Extranjero (NIE) is your Spanish tax identification number as a foreigner. Without it, you cannot sign a title deed, open a Spanish bank account, pay taxes, or set up utility contracts. It is the single most important administrative step before any property transaction, and you should apply for it well before you start making offers.

How to obtain your NIE

If you are still in your home country, you can apply for an NIE at the nearest Spanish consulate or embassy. You will need your passport, a completed EX-15 form, and a fee (currently around €10.50). Processing times vary by consulate — allow four to eight weeks. If you are already in Spain, you can apply at a designated National Police station (Comisaría de Policía) by booking an appointment through the official government website. Alternatively, a legal representative holding a poder notarial (power of attorney) can apply on your behalf, which many buyers find more practical when purchasing from abroad.

Step 2 — Open a Spanish Bank Account

While it is technically possible to transfer the purchase price from a foreign bank account, a local Spanish account is virtually essential for completing the transaction smoothly. Spanish banks require a local account to issue the cheque bancario (banker's draft) used at the notary signing, and you will need it afterwards to pay utility bills, community fees, and annual property taxes via direct debit.

Most major banks — Santander, BBVA, CaixaBank, Sabadell — have dedicated international or non-resident desks. To open an account as a non-resident you typically need your passport and NIE. Some banks also require proof of address in your home country and a statement of the source of funds, particularly for larger transfers. Many clients open their account at the same time as applying for their NIE, treating both tasks as part of the same administrative sprint.

Step 3 — Find Your Property and Instruct an Independent Lawyer

Property portals such as Idealista, Fotocasa, and Rightmove Overseas are the starting point for most international buyers. Once you have identified areas of interest, engaging a local estate agent gives you access to off-market listings and on-the-ground knowledge of realistic pricing.

The single most important decision you will make in the entire process is to instruct your own independent lawyer (abogado) before paying any money. This cannot be overstated. Spanish estate agents represent the seller, and the notary — while a public official — does not investigate the legal status of the property or check for hidden debts. Only your own lawyer does that work on your behalf. Never use the solicitor recommended by the agent or the developer; the conflict of interest is direct and significant.

Step 4 — Due Diligence: The Nota Simple and Beyond

Before any binding contract is signed, your lawyer will conduct a thorough investigation of the property. The starting point is the Nota Simple, an extract from the Land Registry that shows the legal owner, a description of the property, and crucially, any charges, mortgages, or embargoes registered against it.

In Spain, debts attach to the property, not to the person who owes them. If you buy a house with an unpaid mortgage or an outstanding community fee debt, you inherit that liability. A clean Nota Simple is therefore non-negotiable before committing any funds.

Beyond the registry, your lawyer should also check:

  • Municipal planning records to confirm the property has all required building licences and there are no outstanding demolition orders or urban infraction proceedings
  • The Cédula de Habitabilidad (habitation certificate) confirming the property is legally approved for residential use
  • Community of owners accounts to confirm no unpaid maintenance fees
  • IBI (local council tax) receipts to confirm no arrears
  • The cadastral reference to verify the property boundaries match what is on the ground

Step 5 — The Reservation and Arras Contract

Once due diligence is satisfactory, the purchase proceeds in two contractual stages.

The reservation deposit (reserva)

A small initial deposit — typically €3,000 to €6,000 — is paid to take the property off the market for a defined period, usually 14 to 21 days. This buys your lawyer time to finalise due diligence checks and draft the main contract. The reservation agreement should always include conditions under which your deposit is returned in full if legal checks fail.

The contrato de arras

The arras contract is the binding private purchase agreement. You pay 10% of the purchase price (less the reservation already paid) at this stage. This contract is governed by Article 1454 of the Spanish Civil Code, which creates a powerful bilateral commitment: if you as the buyer withdraw from the purchase without just cause, you forfeit the entire 10% deposit. If the seller withdraws, they must return double the deposit — your 10% plus a matching penalty payment. This mutual obligation is what makes the arras such an effective instrument for both parties.

Your lawyer should negotiate protective clauses — for example, making the contract conditional on a satisfactory structural survey, or on mortgage approval if you are financing the purchase. Without such clauses, you risk losing your deposit for reasons outside your control.

Step 6 — Notary Signing and Completion

The final stage of the purchase takes place at the office of a Spanish Notario (Notary Public). Both buyer and seller (or their authorised representatives under a power of attorney) attend to sign the Escritura de Compraventa — the official title deed. The balance of the purchase price is paid by banker's draft at this point. The notary reads the deed aloud, verifies identities, and witnesses the signatures. They do not, however, conduct any legal investigation of the property — that remains entirely your lawyer's responsibility.

If you cannot attend in person — a common situation for buyers purchasing from abroad — your lawyer can attend on your behalf under a poder notarial (power of attorney) granted in your home country (with apostille) or at a Spanish notary during a prior visit to Spain.

Step 7 — Post-Completion: Taxes, Registration, and Utilities

Completion at the notary is not the end of the process. Your lawyer must act quickly on your behalf:

  • Pay the applicable taxes within 30 days — failure to do so attracts surcharges and penalties from the Spanish Tax Authority (Hacienda)
  • Register the deed at the Land Registry — only once registered does ownership transfer fully and officially
  • Switch utilities — water, electricity, gas and broadband need to be transferred to your name with direct debit set up from your Spanish bank account
  • Register the IBI — the annual council tax must be updated to show you as the new owner

The True Cost of Buying Property in Spain

One of the most common mistakes foreign buyers make is budgeting only for the purchase price. In reality, you should set aside an additional 10–12% on top of the purchase price to cover all taxes, fees, and professional costs. Here is a breakdown:

Property Transfer Tax (ITP) — resale properties

If you are buying a second-hand property, you pay the Impuesto de Transmisiones Patrimoniales. The rate is set by each autonomous community: Madrid charges 6%, Andalusia 7%, Valencia 10%, Catalonia 10–11%. For a €300,000 property in Andalusia, that is €21,000 in transfer tax alone.

VAT (IVA) and stamp duty (AJD) — new builds

New build purchases attract IVA at 10% (or 4.5% IGIC in the Canary Islands) plus Actos Jurídicos Documentados (AJD) stamp duty, which ranges from 0.5% to 1.5% depending on the region.

Notary fees

Notary fees are regulated by a fixed scale and typically amount to 0.5–1% of the declared purchase price, depending on the length of the deed and the number of parties involved.

Land Registry fees

Registration at the Land Registry costs approximately 0.4–0.5% of the purchase price.

Legal fees

Independent legal representation costs approximately 1% of the purchase price plus 21% IVA, usually subject to a minimum fee of around €1,500 for straightforward transactions.

Mortgage costs (if applicable)

If you are taking out a Spanish mortgage, budget for a valuation fee (€300–€600) and a mortgage opening commission (0.5–1% of the loan amount). Since the 2019 Ley de Crédito Inmobiliario reform, the bank pays stamp duty and notary fees on the mortgage deed itself.

How the NLV and Property Ownership Work Together

Owning a property in Spain and holding an NLV are natural companions but serve distinct legal purposes. Your NLV is what grants you the right to live in Spain legally as a non-worker; your property ownership gives you a permanent home base. The two reinforce each other in practical terms.

When applying for your NLV, evidence of property ownership in Spain — whether you own outright or are in the process of purchasing — significantly strengthens your application by demonstrating genuine ties to Spain and providing your proof of accommodation without the need for a rental contract. Conversely, once you hold NLV and receive your TIE residence card, Spanish banks treat you as a resident for mortgage purposes, meaning you may access higher loan-to-value ratios and better interest rates than a pure non-resident buyer.

NLV holders are fully entitled to buy property in Spain. There is no conflict between holding an NLV and being a property owner. The visa does not restrict your ability to own assets or receive investment returns.

Best Areas for Expat Property Buyers in Spain

Spain's property market is extraordinarily diverse, and the best area for you depends on your lifestyle priorities, budget, and visa strategy.

Costa del Sol (Andalusia)

The Costa del Sol remains Spain's most popular destination for international buyers, particularly those from the UK, Ireland, Scandinavia, and the United States. Marbella, Estepona, Benahavís, and Nerja offer a mature expat infrastructure, excellent private healthcare, international schools, and year-round sunshine. Property prices range from €150,000 for a coastal apartment to several million for a luxury villa in Marbella's Golden Mile.

Costa Blanca (Valencia region)

Alicante, Torrevieja, Altea, and Jávea attract buyers seeking Mediterranean lifestyle at more accessible price points than the Costa del Sol. The region has excellent transport links, a large established expat community, and competitive property prices — a three-bedroom villa with a pool can often be found for €250,000–€400,000.

The Balearic Islands (Mallorca, Menorca, Ibiza)

The islands command premium prices but offer exceptional quality of life, spectacular scenery, and strong rental income potential. Palma de Mallorca in particular has become a sophisticated hub for affluent international residents. Expect to pay €400,000 upwards for quality property in prime locations.

Interior Spain and rural properties

For buyers seeking space, tranquillity, and outstanding value, inland regions such as Extremadura, Murcia, and Castile offer properties at a fraction of coastal prices. A character farmhouse (finca) with land can be purchased for under €200,000. The due diligence considerations are more complex in rural areas — planning compliance and utility connections require careful checking.
