Spain's Diversity: Why There Is No Single "Best" Location

Spain is a country of extraordinary contrasts. The sun-scorched plains of Castile, the lush green valleys of Galicia, the cosmopolitan energy of Barcelona, the timeless whitewashed villages of Andalusia, and the volcanic landscapes of the Canary Islands are all within the same country. For expats considering a move on a Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV), this diversity is both the great advantage and the great challenge: there is genuinely a place in Spain to suit almost any lifestyle preference, but the choice requires careful self-reflection.

This guide is organised by lifestyle type rather than geography, to help you identify which kind of expat experience aligns with your priorities. Whether you crave city energy, coastal living, peaceful rural life, or island escape, Spain delivers — but the right choice depends on knowing yourself.

For City Lovers: Barcelona, Madrid, Valencia and Seville

Barcelona

Barcelona is one of the world's most iconic cities and consistently ranks as the most popular destination for international expats in Spain. The combination of Gaudí's architecture, world-class food, a vibrant cultural scene, Mediterranean beaches, and an international atmosphere makes it uniquely compelling. The language situation is worth noting: Catalan is the co-official language alongside Spanish, and while all residents speak Spanish, you will encounter Catalan everywhere — on signs, in schools, and in local government communications. English is widely spoken in commercial areas. The trade-off is cost: Barcelona is the most expensive city in Spain, with rents for a two-bedroom apartment in desirable neighbourhoods running €1,500–€2,500/month. NLV holders need to ensure their income comfortably exceeds the minimum threshold to live well here.

Madrid

The capital offers the most complete urban experience in Spain — world-class museums (the Prado, Reina Sofía, Thyssen), outstanding restaurants, a thriving business community, and the best transport links in the country. Madrid sits in the centre of the Iberian plateau at altitude, giving it a continental climate — cold winters and very hot summers are more pronounced than in coastal cities. Rents are slightly lower than Barcelona on average, though premium neighbourhoods (Salamanca, Chamberí) are comparable. For expats who prioritise cultural depth, connectivity, and the full European capital experience, Madrid is the benchmark.

Valencia

Valencia is the increasingly popular "third option" — a genuine Spanish city of 800,000 people with first-rate cultural infrastructure (the City of Arts and Sciences, the IVAM modern art museum), a celebrated food scene, beaches at Malvarrosa, and a cost of living roughly 30–35% below Madrid. The climate is excellent: warm, sunny, and noticeably less extreme than Seville or Madrid in summer. The expat community is growing rapidly — particularly Americans and Canadians drawn by the lifestyle-to-cost ratio. For NLV holders managing their budget carefully, Valencia is one of the most attractive city options in Spain.

Seville

Seville is the soul of Andalusia — flamenco, tapas culture, the Semana Santa processions, and the extraordinary Alcázar and Giralda create an atmosphere unlike anywhere else in Europe. It is hotter than any other major Spanish city in summer (temperatures regularly exceed 40°C in July), which demands honest consideration. Those who manage heat well, or who plan to travel in July and August, will find Seville overwhelmingly beautiful in spring and autumn. The expat community is smaller than in coastal areas but growing, and costs are moderate.

For Coastal Living: Málaga, Alicante and San Sebastián

Málaga

Málaga is the most complete coastal city in Spain for expats — large enough to have excellent urban infrastructure (hospitals, international schools, AVE rail connections, a major airport), yet retaining an authentically Andalusian character. The climate is the warmest in mainland Europe, with over 300 days of sunshine and mild winters. The city itself has undergone dramatic transformation over the past decade: the museum quarter, renovated port area, and vibrant food scene have attracted a sophisticated international community. Property prices and rents are moderate by major-city standards. For expats who want coastal living with real city amenities, Málaga is arguably Spain's best current option.

Alicante

Alicante offers a classic Mediterranean coastal lifestyle at very accessible prices. The city has its own international airport with extensive routes, a historic castle overlooking the bay, excellent beaches (Postiguet beach is right in the city), and a large international community. Day-to-day costs are lower than Málaga, making Alicante an excellent choice for NLV holders looking to stretch their income. The surrounding Costa Blanca offers numerous smaller towns and villages — Jávea, Altea, Moraira — for those who prefer a smaller community within reach of city amenities.

San Sebastián (Donostia)

San Sebastián is for a very specific type of expat: one who prioritises world-class food culture, stunning natural scenery (the bay setting rivals Rio de Janeiro), and a sophisticated, compact city — and who does not require year-round sunshine. The Basque Country has a cool, green, Atlantic climate with mild summers and rainy winters. San Sebastián has more Michelin-starred restaurants per capita than almost anywhere on earth, a thriving surf culture, and a proud Basque identity. Costs are higher than much of Spain. This is not the choice for budget-conscious retirees, but for those seeking something genuinely different, it is extraordinary.

For Rural and Tranquil Living: Extremadura, Castilla and Galicia

A growing number of expats — often disillusioned with urban costs and complexity, or simply craving a different pace — choose rural Spain. The country's interior and northwest offer an authenticity and tranquillity that the popular coastal areas cannot match.

Extremadura (the region bordering Portugal, containing Cáceres and Mérida) is spectacularly beautiful, steeped in history, and extraordinarily affordable. Cáceres is a UNESCO World Heritage city — the medieval centre is one of the best preserved in Europe — and rents are among the lowest in the country. The downside is relative isolation and fewer English-speaking services.

Castilla y León offers historic university cities — Salamanca and Valladolid both have vibrant cultural lives despite their inland locations. Salamanca in particular has a large student population and a cosmopolitan atmosphere that belies its inland, non-coastal setting.

Galicia, in the northwest, is green, rainy, and utterly beautiful — a Celtic-influenced culture with extraordinary seafood, dramatic coastline (the Rías Baixas), and a pace of life completely different from the Mediterranean south. The climate is Atlantic: mild but wet. Santiago de Compostela and Vigo both offer good urban amenities at very low costs.

For Island Living: Canaries, Mallorca and Menorca

Island living in Spain comes in several flavours. The Canary Islands offer the warmest, most stable year-round climate — ideal for retirees and others who need reliable sunshine. Tenerife, Gran Canaria and Lanzarote all have large expat communities, good private healthcare, and regular flights to and from the UK, Germany and Northern Europe. The trade-offs are the island premium on costs and limited flight options in low season.

Mallorca is the most cosmopolitan of the Balearic Islands — Palma de Mallorca is a genuinely sophisticated small city with excellent restaurants, a vibrant arts scene, and a large international community. The island's diversity of landscapes is remarkable. Property prices are among Spain's highest, reflecting demand. The island can feel crowded in summer but is peaceful in winter, when many seasonal businesses close.

Menorca is smaller, quieter, and cheaper than Mallorca — preferred by those seeking genuine tranquillity and a slower pace of island life. It is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, and the pristine beaches and archaeological sites attract a discerning, often returning visitor base.

Language Considerations: English Availability by Region

One practical question that strongly influences location choice for many expats is the availability of English. This varies significantly across Spain:

  • High English availability: Major tourist areas on the Costa del Sol, Costa Blanca, Canary Islands, Balearic Islands; Barcelona (international business community)
  • Good English availability: Málaga city, Alicante city, Valencia, major expat communities in Marbella/Mijas/Torrevieja/Jávea
  • Limited English: Most of inland Spain, rural areas, Galicia, Asturias, Extremadura

If you plan to integrate into Spanish society — learning the language, making Spanish friends, engaging with local life — any region works well. If you need to function primarily in English, the coastal expat hubs and major international cities are more practical choices. It is worth noting that learning even basic conversational Spanish transforms the experience of living anywhere in Spain and opens doors that remain closed to those who don't make the effort.

How the NLV Connects Location to Budget

The Non-Lucrative Visa has a nationwide minimum income requirement (approximately €2,400/month for a single applicant in 2026), but how far that income goes varies enormously by location. In rural Extremadura or inland Castile, €2,400/month provides a genuinely comfortable lifestyle. In Marbella or Barcelona, it covers the basics. When choosing your location, be honest about how much headroom you have above the minimum threshold — and build a realistic monthly budget for your chosen area before committing to the application.
