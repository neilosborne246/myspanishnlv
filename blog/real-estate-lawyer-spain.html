Real Estate Lawyers in Spain: Why You Need One and How to Find a Good One

Why Finding the Right Lawyer Matters More Than Finding the Right Property

This may sound like an overstatement, but many experienced advisors in the Spanish property market genuinely believe it. You can find another property. You cannot easily undo a completed purchase that concealed an unregistered mortgage, illegal building works, or a planning restriction that prevents you from using the property as intended. The right lawyer catches these problems before your money is committed. The wrong lawyer — or no lawyer — leaves you exposed to exactly the risks that have cost foreign buyers tens of thousands of euros in the Spanish market.

This guide focuses specifically on how to find a good real estate lawyer in Spain as a foreign buyer. The complementary article on what property lawyers in Spain actually do covers their role and responsibilities in detail. Here we focus on the selection process: where to look, what to ask, what the different types of legal professional actually are, and what warning signs should make you look elsewhere.

Understanding the Three Key Legal and Administrative Roles

Before you can find the right professional, you need to understand what the different types of legal and administrative practitioners in Spain actually do — and importantly, what they cannot do.

The Abogado (Lawyer / Attorney)

An abogado is a fully qualified lawyer who has completed a Spanish law degree (Licenciatura en Derecho) plus the subsequent master's degree in access to the legal profession (Máster de Acceso a la Abogacía) and passed the state bar examination. They are registered with a regional Colegio de Abogados (Bar Association) and are subject to that body's ethical and professional rules. They carry mandatory professional indemnity insurance.

An abogado can: give you legal advice on contracts and property law; represent you in legal disputes and court proceedings; negotiate contracts on your behalf; provide certified legal opinions; and apply for your NIE and manage your tax obligations in connection with the purchase. For a property purchase as a foreign buyer, you need a qualified abogado — specifically one specialising in real estate law (derecho inmobiliario). This is non-negotiable.

The Gestor (Administrative Manager)

A gestor administrativo is a licensed professional who manages administrative procedures — tax filings, social security, bureaucratic registrations, and compliance. They are regulated by their own professional body (Colegio de Gestores Administrativos). A good gestor is invaluable for ongoing Spanish tax compliance, filing your annual non-resident income tax return (Modelo 210), managing your empadronamiento registration, and handling routine administrative tasks.

However, a gestor is not a lawyer. They cannot give you legal advice on contracts, represent you in disputes, or conduct the legal due diligence required for a property purchase. Some gestorías handle the administrative paperwork at completion — paying taxes, registering at the Land Registry — but this should complement, not replace, the work of your property lawyer. Be wary of anyone marketing themselves as a gestor who claims to offer full legal advice on your property purchase.

The Notario (Notary Public)

The Spanish Notary is a public official appointed by the Ministry of Justice. Their role in a property transaction is to authenticate the title deed (Escritura de Compraventa), verify the identities of the parties, ensure the document meets legal form requirements, and read it aloud at the signing. Since the 2019 mortgage law, they also conduct a binding advisory session with borrowers taking out mortgages, ensuring they understand the terms.

The Notary is strictly neutral. They do not advise either party, they do not investigate the legal history of the property, and they do not protect your interests. Both buyer and seller may use the same Notary, which illustrates their role as a state-appointed authenticator rather than anyone's legal representative. Never confuse the Notary's presence at completion with having your own legal protection in place.

Where to Find a Bilingual Real Estate Lawyer in Spain

For foreign buyers, finding a lawyer who is fluent in your language is not merely a convenience — it is a genuine legal safety measure. A lawyer who can explain every clause of a purchase contract in plain English (or French, German, Dutch, or any other language) eliminates one of the most significant risk factors in cross-border property purchases: misunderstanding what you are agreeing to.

The Colegio de Abogados directories

The most authoritative source of qualified Spanish lawyers is the official registry of the relevant Bar Association. The national body, the Consejo General de la Abogacía Española, maintains a publicly searchable database at abogacia.es. Regional bar associations (Colegios de Abogados) for areas with significant expat populations — Málaga, Alicante, Mallorca, Tenerife — often maintain English-language sections of their websites listing bilingual lawyers by specialism.

Expat community recommendations

Online expat communities — Facebook groups, Reddit communities (r/expats, r/spain), and dedicated forums for specific regions — are a valuable source of genuine, recent recommendations. When someone in a Costa del Sol expat group says "I used this firm for my purchase last year and they were excellent," that recommendation comes from direct recent experience and carries real weight. Look for recommendations that include specific details about the service received, not just a name.

Your home country's embassy or consulate

Many embassies maintain lists of English-speaking lawyers in Spain who have been vetted for basic professional standing. These lists typically do not constitute an endorsement but provide a starting point of practitioners who are at least registered and identifiable.

British, American, and Irish chambers of commerce in Spain

The British Chamber of Commerce in Spain, the American Chamber of Commerce in Spain (AmCham), and the Irish-Spanish Chamber of Commerce all maintain professional directories of member businesses, including law firms specialising in international clients. Membership of these bodies implies a degree of reputational accountability.

The Questions You Must Ask Before Instructing a Lawyer

A confident, competent lawyer will answer all of these questions without hesitation. A lawyer who is evasive, dismissive, or unable to answer clearly should prompt you to look elsewhere.

  • Are you registered with a Spanish Colegio de Abogados? What is your registration number and which college?
  • Do you carry professional indemnity insurance? For what amount?
  • Do you specialise in Spanish real estate law? What proportion of your work involves property purchases?
  • Do you or your firm have any financial relationship with estate agents, developers, or mortgage brokers active in this market?
  • Will you personally handle my file, or will it be delegated to a junior?
  • What specific due diligence checks will you conduct on the property?
  • What is your fee structure, and will you provide a written engagement letter before I instruct you?
  • Can you also assist with my NIE application and tax registration?
  • Do you have experience with NLV applications alongside property purchases?
  • What is your typical communication timeline — how quickly do you respond to client queries?

Red Flags: Signs That This Lawyer Is Not Independent

The legal services market in areas with high concentrations of foreign property buyers has, over the years, attracted some practitioners whose independence is compromised by relationships with other commercial parties. These red flags should prompt you to decline instruction and look elsewhere:

Referral from the estate agent

This is the most common conflict of interest in the Spanish property market. If the estate agent "has a lawyer they always work with," there is an immediate question about whose interests that lawyer primarily serves. Some agents even share office space with their preferred lawyer. Always find your own lawyer independently.

The lawyer also represents the seller

A single lawyer acting for both buyer and seller in the same transaction cannot provide genuinely independent advice to either party. The Spanish Bar Association's ethical rules make this arrangement highly problematic. If a lawyer suggests they can represent both sides of your transaction "to save costs," refuse immediately.

Pressure to proceed quickly or minimise checks

A lawyer who urges you to skip due diligence steps or sign contracts before checks are complete is not acting in your interest. Good legal advice sometimes means slowing down or walking away from a transaction entirely.

Unverifiable credentials

If a lawyer or "legal advisor" cannot or will not provide their Colegio de Abogados registration number, or if that number does not appear in the official registry when you check it, they are not a qualified lawyer. Unregistered "legal advisors" do not carry mandatory professional indemnity insurance and cannot represent you in legal proceedings.

Vague or verbal fee agreements

A reputable lawyer provides a written engagement letter setting out the scope of services and fees before any work begins. Verbal agreements on fees are a red flag — without a written agreement, you have no recourse if the final bill is significantly higher than discussed.

Reluctance to explain their process

A competent, confident property lawyer is transparent about their due diligence process. They should be able to tell you exactly which checks they will run, in what sequence, and what happens at each stage. Vagueness about process is a warning sign that the level of due diligence may not meet the standard you need.

What to Expect in Terms of Cost

Spanish real estate lawyers typically charge on one of two bases: a percentage of the purchase price (usually 1–1.5% plus 21% IVA), or an hourly rate. For routine property purchases, the percentage model is most common. Most reputable firms also establish a minimum fee — typically €1,000–€1,500 — to ensure that the extensive due diligence required is economically viable even for lower-value properties.

On a €250,000 property, expect to pay €2,500–€3,750 plus IVA for independent legal representation. On a €500,000 property, €5,000–€7,500 plus IVA. These figures should be understood not as a cost but as an investment in protecting a very much larger sum.

Always get a written quote before instructing. Ask specifically what is included — NIE assistance, tax filing post-completion, Land Registry inscription — and what might attract additional charges. A transparent, detailed written quote from the outset is itself a positive indicator of how the lawyer will manage your file throughout the transaction.

Your property address is required for your NLV application — specifically, your proof of accommodation in Spain. If you are purchasing rather than renting, the timing of your property completion needs to align with the timing of your NLV application. After Completion: Ongoing Legal Support You May Need

The relationship with your Spanish lawyer does not necessarily end at completion. Depending on your circumstances, you may need ongoing legal support for:

  • NLV renewals — your initial NLV is valid for one year; renewals are typically for two years and then five years, each requiring documentation of continued compliance with visa conditions
  • Spanish will (testamento) — any property owner in Spain should have a Spanish will dealing specifically with their Spanish assets, as international inheritance situations can be complex and expensive to resolve without one
  • Annual tax compliance — non-residents must file an annual non-resident income tax return (Modelo 210) regardless of whether the property generates rental income
  • Rental matters — if you decide to rent your property out, either short-term (holiday lets) or long-term, you will need legal support to ensure compliance with regional licensing requirements and the Spanish Urban Tenancy Act
  • Future sale — when you eventually sell, your lawyer will manage the capital gains tax calculation, the 3% non-resident withholding (if applicable), and the conveyancing process
