Moving to Spain from the USA: Your Complete 2026 Guide

Everything Americans need to know about visas, income requirements, taxes, healthcare and life in Spain.

Why Americans Are Choosing Spain

Spain has emerged as one of the most popular destinations for Americans looking to leave the United States behind. The draw is easy to understand: a high quality of life at a fraction of US costs, 300 days of sunshine in many regions, world-class food, excellent healthcare, and a pace of life that prioritises enjoyment over relentless hustle. Whether you are retiring early, drawing a pension, or living off investment income, Spain offers a compelling package that is hard to match anywhere else in Europe.

The number of Americans living in Spain has grown steadily over the past decade. According to Spain’s National Statistics Institute (INE), tens of thousands of US citizens now hold Spanish residency. That figure accelerated sharply after 2020, as remote-working arrangements, rising US costs of living, and a desire for lifestyle change pushed more Americans to make the leap. Many settle in Madrid or Barcelona for the urban lifestyle; others head to the sun-drenched coasts of Andalusia or the Balearic Islands for a slower pace.

This guide covers everything you need to know before making your move: which visa is right for you, how much income you need, the practical steps after you arrive, the tax implications of living abroad as a US citizen, and how to find your feet in Spanish society.

Which Visa Do You Need? The Non-Lucrative Visa Explained

As a US citizen, you can enter Spain visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period under the Schengen Agreement. For stays longer than 90 days — which means actually living there — you need a long-stay visa before you depart the US.

For most Americans who want to move to Spain without working there, the Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the correct route. The NLV is issued to individuals who can demonstrate sufficient passive income to support themselves in Spain. It is sometimes called the “retirement visa,” though it is not limited to retirees: anyone with investment income, dividends, rental income, pensions, or savings can qualify. The key condition is that you cannot engage in any work or professional activity for Spanish employers or Spanish clients while on this visa.

Other visa options for Americans

If the NLV is not the right fit, other routes include:

  • Digital Nomad Visa (DNV): Launched in 2023, this visa allows remote workers employed by non-Spanish companies to live in Spain. It requires proof of remote income and a contract or client relationship with companies outside Spain.
  • Self-Employment Visa (Autónomo): For Americans who want to set up a business or freelance operation in Spain serving Spanish clients or the Spanish market.
  • Highly Skilled Work Permit: For those with a job offer from a Spanish employer in a skilled role.
  • Student Visa: For full-time study at an accredited Spanish institution.

This guide focuses primarily on the NLV because it is the route chosen by the vast majority of Americans moving to Spain, particularly those who are retired or financially independent.

NLV Income Requirements for US Citizens (2026)

The Spanish government sets the minimum income threshold for the NLV based on a multiple of the IPREM (Indicador Público de Renta de Efectos Múltiples), Spain’s public income reference index. In 2026, the requirements are approximately:

  • Primary applicant: approximately €2,400 per month (around $2,600 at current exchange rates), equivalent to €28,800 per year.
  • Each additional dependent: approximately €600 per month.

These figures represent the 400% IPREM threshold that Spanish consulates apply. The exact amounts can shift slightly each year when the government updates the IPREM, so always verify the current figure with the Spanish consulate in your state.

What counts as income?

Spain accepts a wide range of passive income sources. Acceptable documentation includes:

  • US Social Security benefit statements
  • Private pension or 401(k)/IRA distribution statements
  • Investment portfolio statements (brokerage accounts, ETFs, dividend income)
  • Rental income from US property (with lease agreements)
  • Annuity payment records
  • Bank statements showing consistent balances or regular deposits

You do not need to have all income flowing monthly — consulates also accept evidence of a lump sum in savings sufficient to cover the required amount for the visa period. However, demonstrating regular income rather than static savings is generally more persuasive.

Private health insurance: mandatory

Every NLV application must include proof of comprehensive private health insurance in Spain. The policy must cover the entirety of your stay, have no co-payments (copagos), and not be a travel insurance policy. Premiums typically range from €70 to €200 per month depending on age and coverage level.

Timeline: Planning Your Move from the USA

Moving from the US to Spain is not something you organise in a month. A realistic timeline looks like this:

6 months before your planned move date

  • Decide on your target region and city in Spain
  • Confirm your income meets NLV requirements
  • Begin gathering documents: FBI background check (apostilled), medical certificate, bank statements, pension/income evidence
  • Start researching health insurance policies
  • Notify financial institutions of your planned move

3–4 months before

  • Book consulate appointment (wait times vary significantly by state — some consulates have waits of 2–3 months)
  • Finalise and apostille all documents; arrange sworn Spanish translations
  • Purchase NLV-compliant health insurance policy
  • Research housing in Spain — short-term rental first or long-term lease signed remotely
  • Begin shipping quotes for belongings

1–2 months before

  • Attend consulate appointment and submit NLV application
  • Arrange currency exchange strategy (Wise, OFX, or similar for ongoing transfers)
  • Notify USPS, banks, subscriptions of address change
  • Book flights and finalise shipping

After arrival in Spain

  • Within 30 days: apply for TIE (Tarjeta de Identidad de Extranjero — your residence card) at the local police station
  • Register at the town hall (empadronamiento)
  • Open a Spanish bank account
  • Register with a local health centre if using private health insurance

Your First 30 Days in Spain: Practical Steps

Once you land in Spain with your NLV stamp in your passport, the clock starts on several administrative deadlines. Here is what you must do:

Empadronamiento (municipal registration)

Register your address with the local ayuntamiento (town hall) as soon as you have a fixed address in Spain. You need your passport, your NLV visa, and your rental contract or property deed. The empadronamiento certificate (padrón) is a key document you will use repeatedly — for the TIE application, for accessing healthcare, and for many other administrative purposes. Registration is free and can usually be done without an appointment at smaller town halls.

TIE application (residence card)

Within 30 days of arrival, you must apply for your TIE at the local Oficina de Extranjeros (foreigners’ office) or designated National Police station. The TIE is a biometric card that proves your legal residency in Spain. You will need your passport and visa, padrón certificate, passport photos, Modelo 790 fee payment, and proof of insurance. You will be fingerprinted during the appointment. The card itself is usually collected 4–8 weeks later.

NIE number

Your NIE (Número de Identificación de Extranjero) is your Spanish tax identification number. It is issued as part of the TIE process for NLV holders, though some consulates issue it with the initial visa. You need your NIE to open a bank account, sign a lease, buy property, pay taxes, and conduct most formal transactions in Spain.

Opening a Spanish bank account

Most major Spanish banks — Banco Santander, BBVA, CaixaBank, Bankinter — will open accounts for new residents with your passport, NIE/TIE, padrón certificate, and proof of income. Many branches in large cities have English-speaking staff. For ongoing transfers from the US, services like Wise (formerly TransferWise) offer significantly better exchange rates than traditional bank-to-bank wire transfers.

Cost of Living in Spain vs the USA

One of the most compelling reasons Americans move to Spain is the dramatic difference in cost of living. Across most categories — housing, food, dining out, transport, and healthcare — Spain is significantly cheaper than major US cities.

Housing costs

Rental costs in Spain vary enormously by location. In Madrid or Barcelona’s city centres, a modern one-bedroom apartment rents for €1,200–€1,800 per month — comparable to some US secondary cities. But in Seville, Valencia, Málaga, or the Costa Blanca, a spacious two-bedroom apartment rents for €700–€1,100 per month. Compare that to San Francisco, New York, or Miami, where comparable properties cost three to five times as much. Many American expats report being genuinely shocked at how much more accommodation they get for their money in Spain.

Food and dining

Grocery costs in Spain are lower than in the US: a weekly shop for one person typically costs €50–€80. Eating out is excellent value — a three-course menu del día (set lunch) at a neighbourhood restaurant costs €10–€15 and is a cultural institution. Coffee at a bar costs €1.20–€2.00. Americans consistently report that they eat better and spend less on food in Spain than they did back home.

Healthcare costs

For NLV holders who hold private insurance, out-of-pocket costs are minimal — specialist consultations are often €0 with insurance, compared to hundreds of dollars in the US. After one year of residency, you may access the public healthcare system through the Convenio Especial for a monthly fee of €60–€157, though many Americans prefer to maintain their private policy long-term.

Summary comparison

Overall, most American expats find they can live very comfortably in Spain for €2,000–€3,000 per month as a couple — a standard that would require $6,000–$10,000 per month in cities like San Francisco, New York, or Miami.

Taxes for US Expats in Spain: IRPF, FATCA and FBAR

US tax obligations follow American citizens everywhere in the world — the US taxes based on citizenship, not residence. This means you remain a US taxpayer even after moving to Spain, which creates dual-filing obligations that require careful management.

Spanish income tax (IRPF)

Once you spend more than 183 days per calendar year in Spain, you become a Spanish tax resident and must file a Spanish income tax return (declaración de la renta) declaring your worldwide income. Spanish income tax rates are progressive, ranging from 19% on the first €12,450 to 47% on income over €300,000, though most NLV holders will fall in the lower to middle brackets. Spain also levies a wealth tax (impuesto sobre el patrimonio) on assets above €700,000, though the threshold and rates vary by autonomous community.

US-Spain Double Taxation Treaty

The US and Spain have a Double Taxation Treaty (DTT) signed in 1990. The treaty allocates taxing rights between the two countries for different types of income — US Social Security, for example, is only taxable in the US; Spanish-source income is primarily taxable in Spain. Foreign Tax Credits (FTC) allow you to offset Spanish taxes paid against your US tax bill, preventing most instances of true double taxation. However, the interaction between the two systems is complex and specialist advice from a US-Spain tax professional (a dual-qualified CPA or tax advisor) is strongly recommended.

FATCA and FBAR

US citizens living abroad must comply with two additional reporting requirements:

  • FBAR (FinCEN 114): Required if you hold foreign bank accounts with a combined maximum balance exceeding $10,000 at any point during the year. This includes your Spanish bank account. Filed separately from your tax return via the FinCEN portal.
  • FATCA (Form 8938): Required if your foreign financial assets exceed $200,000 on the last day of the year (or $300,000 at any point). Filed with your 1040.

Penalties for non-compliance are severe, so work with a tax professional familiar with US expat obligations from the outset.

US Social Security in Spain

US Social Security benefits can be paid abroad, including to Spain. Unlike some countries, Spain does not freeze or reduce your Social Security payments. There is a US-Spain Totalization Agreement that prevents double social security taxation and allows some coordination of benefits if you have worked in both countries. Your payments will be made in dollars and transferred to your account.

Healthcare, Driving Licence and Practical Life

Healthcare for NLV holders

Your NLV-compliant private health insurance gives you access to Spain’s excellent private healthcare network. Waiting times are short, English-speaking doctors are readily available in major cities and expat areas, and the standard of care is high. After 12 months of legal residency, you can apply to join the public healthcare system via the Convenio Especial. Many long-term expats do this and maintain private insurance alongside public coverage for added flexibility.

Driving licence

US driving licences are not automatically transferable to Spanish licences — Spain has a bilateral agreement with a very limited number of US states (none of the major ones as of 2026). In practice, most Americans in Spain must take a full Spanish driving test. You can drive on your US licence for up to six months after becoming a Spanish resident, after which you need a Spanish licence. Many expats choose to use public transport or taxis (including apps like Cabify and Uber) rather than go through the test process, particularly in cities.

Language and integration

While English is widely spoken in tourist areas and expat hubs, daily life in Spain is conducted in Spanish. Learning at least conversational Spanish significantly improves your experience and is essential for many administrative dealings. Most cities have language schools and many offer official language classes at subsidised rates for registered residents. Apps like Duolingo, Pimsleur, and italki are popular with expats for accelerating progress.

Popular Destinations for Americans in Spain

Americans tend to gravitate toward a handful of areas, each with its own character:

Barcelona

Cosmopolitan, architecturally stunning, and internationally oriented, Barcelona has the largest English-speaking expat community in Spain. It is the first choice for many Americans who want a big-city experience. Higher cost of living than other Spanish cities, but still much cheaper than comparable US cities.

Madrid

Spain’s capital offers world-class culture, a large international community, excellent transport links, and a dynamic food scene. Many Americans working remotely or in international roles choose Madrid for its amenities and energy.

Málaga and the Costa del Sol

The Costa del Sol has long been the most popular destination for English-speaking expats in Spain. Málaga itself is increasingly popular with younger Americans — a revitalised city with excellent weather, a growing tech scene, and lower costs than Madrid or Barcelona. The surrounding towns of Marbella, Nerja, and Torremolinos offer coastal living with established English-speaking communities.

Seville

Authentically Spanish, significantly cheaper than Madrid or Barcelona, and blessed with outstanding architecture and culture, Seville appeals to Americans who want an immersive Spanish experience. The summer heat (frequently above 40°C) is the main deterrent.

Valencia

Consistently ranked among the best cities in Europe for quality of life, Valencia offers Mediterranean beaches, excellent food, a manageable size, and significantly lower costs than Madrid or Barcelona. Growing rapidly in popularity with American expats.

Shipping your belongings

Most Americans use a combination of air freight for essentials and sea freight for furniture and larger items. Sea freight from the US East Coast to Spain takes approximately 3–5 weeks; from the West Coast, 4–6 weeks. Full-container loads (FCL) cost roughly $3,000–$6,000; less-than-container loads (LCL) are cheaper for smaller shipments. Specialist international removal companies with US-Spain experience include Allied Van Lines International, Rainier Overseas Movers, and several others. Spain imposes no customs duties on household goods for new residents, provided you have held the goods for at least 12 months and can prove Spanish residency.
