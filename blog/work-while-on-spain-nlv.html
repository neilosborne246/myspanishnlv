What Happens If You Work While on the Spain NLV?

The Clear Rule: The NLV Explicitly Prohibits Employment

Let's be direct: the Spain Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) explicitly prohibits employment. This is not a gray area. It's a bright-line rule written into Spanish law. The entire purpose of the NLV is to allow people with passive income to live in Spain without working.

Your NLV approval letter includes a condition that you "will not carry out any activity that constitutes work, profession, business, or self-employment." This is binding. Violating this condition can result in visa cancellation and deportation.

That said, there are important nuances worth understanding.

What Counts as "Work" Under NLV Rules?

Clearly Prohibited:

  • Employment: Being an employee of a Spanish company. Having a Spanish boss, signing an employment contract, receiving a Spanish salary or tax ID — all prohibited.
  • Self-Employment: Operating a business in Spain, even as a freelancer. Having a Spanish tax ID (impuesto sobre actividades económicas — IAE), invoicing Spanish clients, maintaining a Spanish business address — all prohibited.
  • Professional Services: Working as a consultant, lawyer, accountant, doctor, or any professional service provider in Spain. Your professional qualifications don't matter; the activity is prohibited.
  • Teaching: Even if you're a qualified English teacher, formal teaching jobs in Spain are employment and are prohibited. This includes private language schools and tutoring centers that issue official contracts.
  • Running a Business: Starting a bar, restaurant, shop, or any business in Spain is prohibited. Even if you partner with a Spanish citizen, you cannot legally work.

Gray Areas:

Remote Work for Foreign Employers: This is the contentious issue. Many NLV holders work remotely for UK-based companies, US companies, or international clients. Technically, this may violate your visa conditions. Spanish law is ambiguous on whether remote work counts as "work" in Spain if your employer is abroad and you're not physically working in a Spanish office.

The situation is: many people do it, enforcement is inconsistent, and the risk exists but isn't always realized. Some consulates have been more lenient, understanding that remote work is increasingly common and difficult to enforce. However, other consulates are stricter. If caught, you could face visa cancellation.

Passive Income and Investment Management: This is allowed. If you make investment decisions, buy and sell stocks, manage your rental properties, review your pension — these aren't "work" in the employment sense. They're managing your own assets. However, if you become a financial advisor professionally, that's prohibited. The distinction is personal management vs. professional services.

Occasional Consultation: If you provide one-off consultation or advisory work for a foreign company without formal employment or ongoing income, this exists in a gray area. But if it becomes regular income that you depend on, you're essentially working as a consultant, which is prohibited.

Why Is Work Prohibited on the NLV?

Understanding the rationale helps explain the rule:

Spain created the NLV to attract people with independent means — retirees, investors, people with passive income. It's not a work visa. Spain has separate visas for workers (work visas, entrepreneur visas, etc.). The NLV specifically targets people who don't need to work.

By restricting work, Spain protects its domestic job market. It's saying: "We'll welcome foreign retirees and people with investment income, but we won't allow you to take jobs that Spanish workers might fill."

Additionally, restricting work ensures that NLV holders genuinely have the income they claimed. If you could work, the income requirement becomes less meaningful — anyone could move to Spain and find employment there, regardless of their passive income. The income requirement verifies financial security.

Realistic Enforcement: How Strictly Is This Enforced?

Here's the uncomfortable truth: enforcement is inconsistent.

Scenarios where enforcement is likely:

  • If you register for a Spanish tax ID (impuesto sobre actividades económicas — IAE) as self-employed, you're openly declaring you're working. This is likely to trigger action.
  • If you work as an employee of a Spanish company with an employment contract, Spanish payroll taxes, and a Spanish employer reporting you to authorities, this will be discovered.
  • If someone reports you — a disgruntled business partner, a former employer, someone you've angered — authorities may investigate.
  • If your income sources suddenly change and you're paying taxes on employment income rather than passive income, this can trigger questions during renewal or tax audits.

Scenarios where enforcement is unlikely:

  • If you work remotely for a foreign employer, and no one reports you, and you don't change your tax status or income sources, authorities won't discover this unless they specifically investigate.
  • If you do occasional unpaid volunteer work, this isn't "work" in the employment sense — it's community involvement.
  • If you manage your own investments and rental properties, this isn't employment — it's passive income management.

The reality: Many NLV holders work remotely. Some get away with it indefinitely. Some get caught during renewal when consulates ask detailed questions about income sources. Some face no consequences because enforcement is inconsistent and depends on whether someone reports them.

However, understanding that enforcement is inconsistent is not the same as saying the rule doesn't matter. It exists, violations carry real penalties, and you're taking a risk if you violate it.

What Happens If You're Caught Working?

Best Case: You're warned and told to stop. Your next renewal might be denied, but you're not immediately deported.

More Likely: Your visa is cancelled. You're required to leave Spain. You may face deportation proceedings and be banned from re-entering Spain for a period.

Worst Case: You're prosecuted for fraud (if you misrepresented your income on your application), deported, and banned from Spain indefinitely.

The consequences are serious. This isn't a small fine; this is loss of your residency and ability to live in Spain.

If You Need or Want to Work: What Are Your Options?

Option 1: Get a Different Visa

If you need to work in Spain, apply for a work visa instead. Spain has several:

  • Work Visa: For employment by a Spanish employer. Your employer must sponsor you.
  • Entrepreneur Visa: For starting a business in Spain. You must show business plan, financial investment, and job creation.
  • Digital Nomad Visa: Some regions (e.g., Lisbon, Barcelona area companies) are experimenting with digital nomad visas for remote workers. These are still developing, but they may legalize what many NLV holders currently do in a gray area.
  • Freelancer Visa: For self-employed professionals in certain regulated fields.

These visas have different requirements and complexity, but they're the legal path if you want to work.

Option 2: Choose the Digital Nomad Visa Instead

Working remotely for foreign employers is not allowed on the NLV — the regulation makes no exception for them (RD 1155/2024, art. 61.1). If remote work is your income, the Digital Nomad Visa is the route designed for it.

Option 3: Live on Your Declared Income

If you have passive income meeting the NLV threshold, you're supposed to live on this. It's not always adequate for the lifestyle you want, but it's legal and secure.

Option 4: Return to Your Home Country Periodically for Work

If you have flexibility, you could spend part of the year in Spain on your NLV, and part of the year in your home country or elsewhere working. This keeps your NLV compliant while allowing you to earn more income. It requires flexibility in lifestyle, though.

Tax Implications of Working on the NLV (If You Do)

If you work remotely, you have tax obligations:

In Spain: If you're a Spanish tax resident (183+ days/year in Spain), you must report worldwide income to Spanish authorities. Working remotely for a foreign employer creates income you must declare. Failure to declare is tax evasion — a separate offense from visa violations.

In Your Home Country: If you're a UK citizen, you're not a UK tax resident once you're non-resident in Spain. However, UK-source income might be taxable in the UK. Complex double-taxation considerations apply.

Many people who work remotely on an NLV don't properly file taxes — another risk alongside the visa violation.

The Honest Answer to "Can I Work on the NLV?"

The legal answer: No, absolutely not. The visa explicitly prohibits it.

The practical answer: Many people do remote work anyway, enforcement is inconsistent, and you might get away with it. But you're accepting risk: visa cancellation, deportation, and tax penalties.

The recommendation: If you need to work, get a work visa. If you're considering the NLV despite needing some income, think carefully about whether this is sustainable. Remote work is not an exception: the NLV authorises you to live in Spain without carrying out work or professional activities (RD 1155/2024, art. 61.1).

The safest path: Live on your declared passive income and don't work. It's compliant, secure, and you won't lose your residency. If your passive income isn't sufficient, reconsider whether the NLV is the right visa for you.

Changing From the NLV to a Work Permit

If your plans change and you want to work in Spain, the NLV itself never allows it — but the regulation lets someone living in Spain on a temporary residence permit apply for a residence and work permit without returning home for a new visa (RD 1155/2024, art. 191).

  • Less than a year on your permit: you must meet all the requirements for an initial work permit, including the job-market test, and the new permit lasts one year (art. 191.2).
  • At least a year on a permit that did not allow work (such as the NLV): the job-market test does not apply; the new one-year permit takes effect once you are registered with Social Security, which must happen within a month of the decision (art. 191.4).

Owning shares or a company vs working in it

Owning shares is compatible with the NLV; working in the business is not. If your funds come from shares in a Spanish company, you must certify that you do not work in it and sign a declaration to that effect (RD 1155/2024, art. 62.3). Dividends from a company abroad count as passive income — see dividend income and the NLV.
