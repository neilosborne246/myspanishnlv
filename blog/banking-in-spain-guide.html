Banking in Spain: How to Open a Bank Account as a Foreigner

Everything you need to know about choosing the right Spanish bank, the documents required, and managing your money from abroad.

Why NLV Holders Need a Spanish Bank Account

Opening a Spanish bank account is one of the most practical steps you can take after arriving in Spain on a Non-Lucrative Visa. While it is technically possible to live in Spain using only a foreign bank card, day-to-day life becomes significantly more complicated without a local account.

A Spanish bank account with a Spanish IBAN is required for setting up direct debits — this includes rent payments, utility bills, mobile phone contracts, and internet services. Many Spanish landlords will not accept payment from a foreign account, and some utility providers simply cannot process overseas IBANs at all. Having a local account also makes it far easier to pay taxes, deal with Spanish administrative bodies, and demonstrate financial activity in Spain — all of which matters when renewing your NLV.

NLV holders are not permitted to work in Spain, so your income will arrive from abroad — whether that is a pension, investment returns, rental income, or savings. A Spanish account makes receiving and managing those funds much simpler and usually cheaper than constant international transfers to a foreign card.

Non-Resident vs Resident Bank Accounts in Spain

When you first arrive in Spain — or if you want to open an account before you move — you have the option of a non-resident bank account (cuenta de no residente). This type of account is available to anyone who does not hold Spanish residency. It allows you to make payments, receive transfers, and hold funds in euros, but it typically comes with fewer features and occasionally higher fees than a resident account.

Once you have your TIE (Tarjeta de Identidad de Extranjero) — the residence card you collect in Spain after your NLV is approved — you can convert your non-resident account to a full resident account. This usually involves a simple visit to your bank branch with your TIE and NIE. Resident accounts tend to have better terms: lower fees, access to more products, and wider acceptance for automated payments.

If you are applying for your NLV from outside Spain, it can be worth opening a non-resident account in advance. This allows you to demonstrate financial activity in Spain and makes the transition smoother when you arrive.

Documents You Need to Open a Spanish Bank Account

The documents required depend on whether you are opening a non-resident or resident account. Spanish banks are also subject to anti-money laundering regulations, so they will carry out checks on the source of your funds.

For a Non-Resident Account

  • Valid passport (or national identity card for EU citizens)
  • Proof of address in your home country — a recent utility bill or bank statement, ideally less than three months old
  • Proof of employment or income status — a payslip, pension statement, tax return, or investment portfolio statement
  • Non-resident certificate — some banks require a letter confirming you are not a Spanish tax resident; this can be obtained from a local police station in Spain or from your consulate

Documents from abroad typically need to be translated into Spanish by a sworn (official) translator if they are not already in Spanish. Plan ahead for this if you are opening an account remotely.

For a Resident Account

  • Valid passport
  • NIE number (Número de Identificación de Extranjero)
  • TIE card confirming your residency status
  • Proof of Spanish address — a rental contract, utility bill, or certificate of empadronamiento (municipal registration)
  • Proof of income or financial means

The Best Banks in Spain for Foreigners

Spain has a well-developed banking sector with several major national banks and a growing number of digital-only options. The right choice depends on where you live in Spain, how much support you need in English, and whether you prefer in-branch or online banking.

Santander

Santander is one of the largest banks in the world and has an enormous branch network across Spain. It is a solid choice for NLV holders who want face-to-face service and who may already be familiar with Santander in the UK, Ireland, or Latin America. Its Cuenta Mundo account is specifically designed for new residents and foreign nationals, with no account fees for the first year. English-speaking staff are available in branches in major cities and expat areas.

BBVA

BBVA is Spain's second largest bank and is widely regarded as having one of the best mobile banking apps in Europe. It is particularly popular among younger expats and digitally confident users. BBVA offers a straightforward account opening process for non-residents and has English-language support through its app and phone line. It also allows you to open an account entirely online in some circumstances.

CaixaBank

CaixaBank is the largest bank by domestic market share in Spain and has the widest branch network in the country. This makes it especially useful if you are living outside a major city. Its CaixaBankNow app is well-regarded, and the bank has English-speaking staff in areas with large expat populations such as the Costa del Sol, Costa Blanca, and the Balearics.

Sabadell

Banco Sabadell has made a deliberate effort to attract British expats in particular, following its acquisition of TSB in the UK (later sold). It offers a Cuenta Expansión designed for international clients and has English-language services in many areas popular with British retirees. Its fees are competitive and it has a strong presence in Catalonia, Valencia, and Andalusia.

Openbank

Openbank is the fully digital bank owned by Santander. It charges no monthly fees, offers a free debit card, and can be managed entirely online or via app. It is an excellent option for NLV holders who are comfortable with digital banking and want to avoid branch visits. Openbank can be opened remotely before you arrive in Spain, making it popular with those preparing their move from abroad. Note that it requires a Spanish NIE.

N26

N26 is a German digital bank operating across Europe. It issues a Spanish IBAN, has no monthly fees on its basic account, and can be opened entirely via the app. It is well-suited to expats who want a simple, modern banking experience and who are used to managing finances digitally. However, N26 does not have physical branches, so it is best used alongside another account for any in-person needs. Note that N26 requires you to be a resident of Spain (with a Spanish address) to open an account.

How to Open a Spanish Bank Account: Step by Step

Whether you choose a traditional bank or a digital option, the general process is similar.

  1. Choose your bank based on location, language support, fees, and digital features.
  2. Gather your documents — passport, proof of address, proof of income, and NIE if you have one.
  3. Apply online or visit a branch. Many banks now allow online applications, but non-residents often need to complete at least one in-person verification step.
  4. Complete identity verification. This may involve a video call, a visit to a branch, or uploading documents via the bank's app.
  5. Sign the contract — usually done digitally via an SMS verification code.
  6. Set up online banking access with your chosen passwords and authentication methods.
  7. Activate your debit card once it arrives by post, usually within five to ten working days.

If you are opening an account remotely before your move, digital banks like Openbank or N26 offer the smoothest process. For traditional banks, some branches — particularly in cities like Madrid, Barcelona, and Málaga — can open accounts via video appointment.

Online Banking Options for NLV Holders

Digital-only banks have become increasingly practical for expats in Spain. Beyond Openbank and N26 mentioned above, Revolut is widely used by expats for day-to-day spending, currency exchange, and international transfers. However, Revolut issues a Lithuanian IBAN rather than a Spanish one, which means it cannot be used for some Spanish direct debits or utility payments.

If you plan to use Revolut or a similar fintech for spending money, it is wise to also hold a Spanish-IBAN account (such as Openbank or N26) for paying rent, utilities, and setting up Spanish direct debits. Many expats use a combination: a digital bank for international transfers and daily spending, and a Spanish high-street bank for local obligations.

Transferring Money to Spain from Abroad

As an NLV holder, your income arrives from outside Spain — so managing international transfers efficiently is important. Traditional banks typically charge 2–4% in hidden exchange rate margins plus flat fees, which adds up significantly over time.

Specialist transfer services offer much better rates:

  • Wise (formerly TransferWise) — widely regarded as the gold standard for expat transfers. Uses the mid-market exchange rate and charges a small, transparent percentage fee. Transfers to a Spanish account typically arrive same-day or next-day.
  • OFX — good for larger transfers, with dedicated account managers for high-value transactions such as pension transfers.
  • Currencies Direct — popular with expats, particularly for regular monthly transfers. Offers forward contracts to lock in exchange rates.
  • TorFX — well-regarded for larger or more complex transfers, with a personal account manager service.

For regular monthly transfers — such as receiving a pension or investment income — setting up a regular payment plan with a currency specialist can save hundreds or thousands of euros per year compared to using your home country bank.

Banking Tips for NLV Holders in Spain

Here are some practical points specific to NLV holders:

  • Get your NIE before opening an account. While some banks will accept a passport alone for non-resident accounts, most prefer or require a NIE. Obtaining your NIE should be one of your first administrative steps after arriving.
  • Register at the town hall (empadronamiento) as soon as possible. Your certificate of empadronamiento serves as proof of Spanish address and is widely accepted by banks.
  • Keep your account active. Banks may close dormant accounts. Make sure some transactions flow through your Spanish account regularly, even if just paying a utility bill.
  • Declare your foreign accounts to the Spanish tax authority. Once you become a Spanish tax resident (after spending more than 183 days in Spain in a calendar year), you are required to declare overseas bank accounts holding more than €50,000 using the Modelo 720 form. Speak to a gestor or tax advisor about this.
  • Be aware of the Modelo 347 and other reporting requirements if you receive large transfers. Your bank may request documentation for transfers above certain thresholds.
