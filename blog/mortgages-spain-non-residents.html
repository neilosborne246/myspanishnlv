Getting a Mortgage in Spain as a Non-Resident: 2026 Guide

Everything non-EU and non-resident buyers need to know about LTV limits, lenders, rates, documents, and how obtaining an NLV dramatically improves your mortgage terms.

Can Non-Residents Get a Mortgage in Spain?

Yes — and more straightforwardly than many people expect. Spain's major banks actively market mortgage products to non-resident international buyers, and the process, while requiring more documentation than a resident application, is well-trodden territory. Foreign nationals from the UK, US, Canada, Australia, and beyond have been successfully financing Spanish property purchases for decades.

The critical distinction is between how banks treat non-residents (those who spend fewer than 183 days per year in Spain and whose primary tax residence is elsewhere) versus residents (those registered as Spanish tax residents). Non-residents face more conservative lending criteria: lower loan-to-value ratios, slightly higher interest rate spreads, and stricter income documentation requirements. But the access to mortgage finance is real and accessible.

This distinction also creates a powerful financial incentive for buyers planning to move to Spain on an NLV. Once you obtain your visa, move to Spain, and receive your TIE residence card, Spanish banks reclassify you as a resident for mortgage purposes — unlocking better LTV ratios, lower rate spreads, and longer maximum terms. For NLV applicants who are planning to finance their property purchase, the timing of the mortgage application relative to the NLV application matters significantly.

Loan-to-Value Limits for Non-Residents

The most significant practical difference for non-resident buyers is the LTV cap. Spanish banks typically lend non-residents no more than 60–70% of the lower of the purchase price or the bank's own valuation of the property. This means you must fund 30–40% from your own resources — and that is before the additional 10–12% required to cover taxes and professional fees.

On a €350,000 property purchase, a non-resident buyer at 70% LTV needs:

  • 30% deposit: €105,000
  • 10% taxes and fees (approximate): €35,000
  • Total minimum own funds: €140,000

The bank's valuation may differ from the purchase price. If the bank values the property at €330,000 but you are paying €350,000, the bank's 70% LTV is based on €330,000 (giving a loan of €231,000), not the purchase price — meaning you need to fund the difference in addition to the 30% deposit.

The Main Lenders for Non-Resident Mortgages

Spain's major banks all offer non-resident mortgage products, though their terms, documentation requirements, and appetite for different buyer profiles vary. Here is a brief overview of the main players:

Santander

One of the most internationally experienced lenders for foreign buyers, Santander offers dedicated non-resident mortgage products with online application tools and English-speaking relationship managers in areas with significant expat populations. Their Hipoteca No Residente product offers fixed, variable, and mixed rate options.

BBVA

BBVA actively markets to non-resident buyers through its international digital channels. The application process is largely digital and BBVA publishes English-language mortgage information. Typical LTV for non-residents is 70%, dropping to 65% for certain third-country nationalities depending on compliance requirements.

Sabadell

Sabadell has historically been one of the most active lenders to the British expat market (including through its former subsidiary TSB) and maintains strong capability with English-speaking buyers. It publishes step-by-step guides for non-resident applicants and offers competitive rates for strong profiles.

CaixaBank

CaixaBank's HolaBank service is specifically designed for international clients, offering multilingual support, a dedicated international client team, and streamlined documentation review. It is particularly well-regarded for managing applications from buyers in the US, Canada, and Northern Europe.

Bankinter

Bankinter offers a mixed-rate mortgage product specifically marketed to non-residents who want the security of a fixed initial period combined with potential variable savings later. It is worth comparing their terms on mixed products if you plan to refinance or sell within ten years.

Interest Rates in 2026: Fixed, Variable, and Mixed

The Spanish mortgage market in 2026 is operating against the backdrop of a declining Euribor. The 12-month Euribor — the benchmark for virtually all variable-rate Spanish mortgages — has eased from its 2023 peak of over 4% and now sits in the 2.5–3% range, with analysts expecting continued gradual decline through 2026.

Fixed-rate mortgages

Fixed rates for non-residents on 20-year terms are currently in the 3.2–3.6% range, depending on the lender, your profile, and the LTV. Fixed rates provide certainty — your monthly payment never changes regardless of Euribor movements — which is particularly valuable if your income is in a non-euro currency and you need to budget in euros.

Variable-rate mortgages

Variable rates are set as Euribor plus a spread. For non-residents, typical spreads are Euribor + 1.5–2.0%. At a current Euribor of around 2.5%, that places the all-in rate at 4.0–4.5%. Variable rates benefit from any future Euribor declines but expose you to rate risk if rates rise again.

Mixed-rate mortgages

Mixed (or hybrid) mortgages have seen significant growth in Spain since 2024. These typically fix the rate for an initial period (five to fifteen years) then revert to a variable rate (Euribor + spread) for the remainder. For buyers who want early certainty but believe rates will fall further over their holding period, a mixed mortgage offers an interesting balance. Rates for the fixed period are typically slightly above pure fixed rate offers but below long-term fixed comparisons when blended over the full term.

Documentation Requirements for Non-Resident Applicants

Non-resident mortgage applications require more documentation than resident applications, and the documentation requirements are more stringent for buyers from outside the EU. Allow plenty of time to gather and apostille documents — rushed applications with missing paperwork are a frequent cause of delays that in turn cause buyers to miss their arras completion deadlines.

Standard documentation requirements include:

  • NIE number — mandatory for all Spanish financial transactions
  • Passport — certified copy, sometimes notarised
  • Employment contract or proof of self-employment — with certified translation
  • Last 3–6 months' payslips or two years' tax returns for self-employed applicants
  • Last 3–6 months' bank statements from your primary account
  • Credit report from your home country — Experian, Equifax, TransUnion, or local equivalent
  • Home country tax returns for the last two years, with apostille for non-EU applicants
  • Proof of home address — utility bill or equivalent
  • Signed purchase contract (reservation or arras) for the Spanish property
  • For US citizens specifically — Form W-9 and IRS transcripts (FATCA compliance requirement)

All foreign-language documents must be translated by a sworn translator (traductor jurado). Documents from outside Spain typically require a Hague Apostille. Many lenders will begin a preliminary assessment on the basis of copies but require originals before issuing a binding mortgage offer.

Broker vs. Going Direct to a Bank

You have two main routes to a Spanish mortgage as a non-resident: approaching banks directly, or using a mortgage broker who specialises in Spanish non-resident finance.

Going direct to a bank gives you access to that bank's products without a broker fee, and if you already have a relationship with a particular bank (for example, Santander operates in many countries), that existing relationship may smooth the process. The disadvantage is that you are seeing only that bank's products and are likely to spend significant time on the application before knowing whether that bank's appetite matches your profile.

A specialist Spanish mortgage broker has access to multiple lenders' products, understands which banks have the strongest appetite for your particular profile and nationality, can pre-screen your application to identify the most likely approvals, and manages the documentation process across potentially complex requirements. Brokers typically charge 0.5–1% of the loan amount as a fee, which they may receive from the lender rather than the buyer in some structures.

For buyers with straightforward profiles — salaried income, EU nationality, clean credit history — going direct can work well. For more complex profiles — self-employed income, non-EU nationality, variable income sources — a broker's market knowledge typically pays for itself.

The NLV Advantage: How Residency Transforms Your Mortgage Options

One of the most practical financial benefits of obtaining the Non-Lucrative Visa and receiving your TIE residence card is the immediate reclassification by Spanish banks from non-resident to resident borrower status. This is not merely a technicality — it translates directly into better lending terms:

  • LTV increases from 60–70% to up to 80%, reducing the minimum deposit requirement by 10–20 percentage points of the purchase price
  • Interest rate spreads decrease by typically 0.3–0.5 percentage points on variable rates
  • Maximum term extends from 25 years to 30 years in many cases
  • Debt-to-income tolerance increases slightly, potentially allowing a larger loan relative to income

On a €400,000 property, the difference between a 70% LTV non-resident mortgage and an 80% LTV resident mortgage is €40,000 in minimum required deposit — a significant real-world financial difference. For buyers who are applying for NLV at the same time as purchasing a property, it may be worth considering whether delaying the mortgage application until after TIE receipt is financially advantageous, if the seller is willing to agree a longer completion timeline.

Important Protections Under the 2019 Mortgage Law

Spain's Ley de Crédito Inmobiliario (Law 5/2019) introduced significant consumer protections that benefit all mortgage borrowers, including non-residents. Key provisions include:

  • The bank pays stamp duty (AJD), notary fees, and Land Registry fees on the mortgage deed — previously paid by the borrower
  • Mandatory minimum reflection period of ten days between the bank issuing the binding offer and the signature date
  • Caps on early repayment fees — 0.25% of outstanding balance in the first three years on variable mortgages, 0% thereafter
  • Requirements for transparent comparison of fixed vs. variable products before signature
  • Compulsory independent notary advice session for the borrower before signing

These protections reduce the upfront cost of taking a mortgage in Spain and provide stronger rights than the pre-2019 regime. The bank now covering AJD and notary fees saves a typical borrower €2,000–€4,000 compared to the previous rules.
