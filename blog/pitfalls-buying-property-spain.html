10 Pitfalls to Avoid When Buying Property in Spain

The most costly mistakes foreign buyers make in the Spanish property market — and how to protect yourself from every one of them.

Why Foreign Buyers Are Particularly Vulnerable

Spain's property market is transparent, well-regulated, and genuinely attractive for international buyers. But that regulation only protects you if you know how to use it — and many foreign buyers, unfamiliar with Spanish legal procedures and operating in an unfamiliar language, walk into the market without the knowledge or professional support they need. The result is that mistakes that would never happen to an experienced local buyer become alarmingly common among international purchasers.

None of the ten pitfalls listed below are inevitable. Every single one is avoidable with proper preparation, good legal advice, and a refusal to be rushed into decisions before due diligence is complete. Consider this guide your checklist — and share it with anyone you know who is considering buying in Spain.

Pitfall 1: Buying Without an Independent Lawyer

This is the root cause of most of the other pitfalls on this list. Every serious error that foreign buyers make in Spain traces back, directly or indirectly, to proceeding without independent legal representation.

The Spanish Notary is not your lawyer. The estate agent works for the seller. The developer's legal team works for the developer. In Spain, nobody in the transaction automatically acts in your interest unless you hire them specifically to do so. Without an independent lawyer reviewing the title, checking planning compliance, verifying there are no debts attached to the property, and negotiating contract terms on your behalf, you are entirely exposed.

How to avoid it: Instruct an independent, registered abogado before paying any money — before the reservation deposit, before the arras. Verify their registration with the relevant Colegio de Abogados. Never use the lawyer recommended by the estate agent or developer.

Pitfall 2: Buying Illegal or Unlicensed Property

This is Spain's most infamous property pitfall, and it is far more common than most buyers realise — particularly in rural areas and coastal regions that experienced rapid, poorly regulated development in the 1980s and 1990s. In some areas of Andalusia, Murcia, and the Valencia region, a significant proportion of rural properties have unlicensed structures: extensions built without planning permission, pools without building licences, entire outbuildings with no legal existence, or in the most extreme cases, houses built on land classified as non-developable agricultural land.

If you buy a property with illegal building works, you inherit the liability. Depending on when the works were built and whether any statute of limitations has expired (in some cases illegal works become immune to demolition orders after a number of years), the consequences range from the need to regularise the works at your expense to an active demolition order requiring you to tear down the structure.

How to avoid it: Your lawyer must check the property's planning history with the local town hall (ayuntamiento) before you sign anything. They will verify that all structures are either licensed or benefit from a prescription period. This check is non-negotiable and must be completed before the arras.

Pitfall 3: Inheriting the Seller's Debts

In Spain, debts attach to the property, not to the person who owes them. This is one of the most fundamental — and most surprising — principles of Spanish property law for foreign buyers. It means that if the seller owes unpaid community maintenance fees, council tax arrears (IBI), an outstanding mortgage, or utility debts attached to the property, those debts become your legal responsibility the moment you complete the purchase.

The consequences can be severe. A community of owners with significant unpaid fees can pursue the new owner for those fees immediately after purchase. The local municipality can enforce IBI arrears against the property through a charge. An undisclosed mortgage can surface at the Land Registry and cloud your title.

How to avoid it: Your lawyer must obtain: a certificate from the community of owners confirming no outstanding fees; IBI payment receipts for the last five years; a confirmed Land Registry search (Nota Simple) showing no mortgages or charges; and confirmation from the seller that all utilities are paid up. Any outstanding amounts must be deducted from the completion payment or cleared before signing.

Pitfall 4: Not Understanding Planning Permission Rules

Spain has a complex and sometimes inconsistent planning framework that operates at national, regional, and municipal levels. Land is classified into different categories — urban, urbanisable, and non-urbanisable (including agricultural, protected, and coastal land) — with radically different rules about what can be built where. The coastal law (Ley de Costas) imposes strict limitations on building within certain distances of the coastline. Regional laws add further layers.

The practical consequence for buyers is that a property that looks entirely legitimate and has been occupied for years may nonetheless have structural compliance problems — particularly if it was built during a period of lax enforcement, or if planning rules have changed since construction. In some coastal areas, properties within the public maritime zone face restrictions that affect their long-term legal security.

How to avoid it: Do not assume that an occupied, marketed property is automatically planning-compliant. Your lawyer must check the full planning history, not just the Land Registry. For rural and coastal properties, this check is especially important.

Pitfall 5: Failing to Check for Encumbrances and Charges

Beyond mortgages and community fee debts, Spanish properties can be subject to a range of legal encumbrances that are not immediately obvious but can significantly affect your ownership rights or ability to sell in the future. These include: servitudes (rights of way across your land for neighbours or utilities), embargos (court-ordered attachments from legal proceedings against the seller), and pre-emption rights (derechos de tanteo y retracto) allowing certain parties to buy the property ahead of any third-party purchaser in specific circumstances.

The Nota Simple from the Land Registry should reveal registered charges, but it may not capture all encumbrances — particularly informal rights of way or unregistered servitudes that have been exercised for years without formal registration.

How to avoid it: A thorough Land Registry search is essential. For rural properties, your lawyer should also investigate any informal access arrangements, shared water rights, or agricultural easements. Physical inspection of the boundaries with attention to any tracks, cables, or pipes crossing the land is advisable.

Pitfall 6: Not Budgeting for the True Total Cost

One of the most predictable financial shocks for first-time buyers in Spain is discovering the true total cost of purchase after they have already committed to the price. The purchase price is only the starting point. Buyers who budget only for the headline figure routinely find themselves short of funds at completion or facing unexpected financial strain in the months after moving in.

The additional costs to budget for on top of the purchase price include: property transfer tax (ITP) at 6–11% depending on region and property type; notary fees (0.5–1%); Land Registry fees (0.4–0.5%); independent legal fees (~1%); bank account opening and wire transfer costs; bank valuation fee for mortgage buyers (€300–€600); and if financing, mortgage opening commission (0.5–1% of loan). For a €300,000 property in Andalusia, the total additional costs typically run to €33,000–€36,000.

How to avoid it: Budget a minimum of 10–12% on top of the agreed purchase price for all taxes and fees before you make any offer. Ask your lawyer for a detailed cost breakdown before signing the arras.

Pitfall 7: Signing the Arras Contract Too Quickly

The contrato de arras is presented by many estate agents as a routine, quick formality — sign it now to secure the property. This is one of the most dangerous misconceptions in the Spanish property market. The arras is a binding legal contract with significant financial consequences for withdrawal. Signing it before due diligence is complete, or signing a standard template without your lawyer's review and amendment, exposes you to serious financial risk.

Estate agents — who work for the seller and earn commission on completion — have a financial interest in getting the arras signed as quickly as possible. This does not mean they are dishonest, but their interest and your interest are not the same. The pressure to sign quickly can be considerable, particularly in competitive markets where other buyers may be interested.

How to avoid it: Never sign any contract — including a reservation contract — without your lawyer's review. Instruct your lawyer before you start viewing properties, so they are ready to act quickly when you find the right one. A good lawyer can typically turn around an arras review within 48–72 hours.

Pitfall 8: Ignoring the Need for a Structural Survey

Unlike the UK, where the RICS survey is a well-established part of the purchase process, Spain has no equivalent cultural norm of commissioning structural surveys before purchase. Many buyers — particularly those purchasing apartments in established developments — proceed without any independent structural assessment. For newer properties and apartments in well-maintained buildings, this may be a reasonable judgement. For older properties, rural fincas, or properties with visible signs of deferred maintenance, skipping a survey can be an expensive mistake.

Common issues found in structural surveys on Spanish properties include: damp penetration (particularly in basements and north-facing walls); flat roof problems; structural cracking; outdated electrical wiring that requires replacement to meet current standards; and, in some coastal areas, concrete deterioration caused by salt air (carbonatación).

How to avoid it: For any property more than fifteen years old, or any rural property, commission an independent structural survey by a registered Spanish architect or surveyor before the arras. Ensure the arras includes a condition allowing withdrawal if the survey reveals defects above a defined cost threshold.

Pitfall 9: The Risks of Off-Plan Purchases Without Guarantees

Buying off-plan — committing to a property during or before construction — is a perfectly legitimate and often financially attractive strategy. New developments in coastal areas often offer lower entry prices during the pre-sale phase, and buyers who complete several years later may benefit from significant price appreciation. But the risks specific to off-plan purchases require additional protections that many buyers overlook.

The primary off-plan risk is developer insolvency. Spain's property market history includes periods where developers took stage payments, ran into financial difficulties, and left buyers without their property and without their money. Spanish law requires that developer-held stage payments be protected by bank guarantees (avales bancarios) or insurance bonds, but enforcement has been inconsistent and buyers who do not specifically demand these guarantees in writing can find themselves without protection.

How to avoid it: Before paying any stage payment on an off-plan purchase, confirm in writing that your payments are covered by a valid bank guarantee or insurance bond. Your lawyer must verify the guarantee is in place for each payment. Also ensure the purchase contract includes specific penalty clauses for late delivery and defines the specification precisely.

Pitfall 10: Assuming Property Ownership Grants Residency

This is perhaps the most emotionally charged pitfall on the list, because it affects not just the financial investment but the entire life plan. Many international buyers purchase a property in Spain with the assumption — sometimes explicitly reinforced by estate agents eager to close a sale — that ownership of a Spanish property either automatically grants residency rights or makes residency very easy to obtain.

This is false. For non-EU nationals (including UK citizens post-Brexit, Americans, Canadians, Australians, and all other non-EEA nationals), owning property in Spain does not grant any form of residency right beyond the standard 90-day Schengen visitor rule. An expensive villa does not entitle you to live in it year-round any more than an €80,000 apartment. To live legally in Spain as a non-EU national, you must hold a valid visa or residence permit.

For most property-buying expats, the Non-Lucrative Visa is the answer. It allows you to live in Spain for renewable one-year periods on the basis of passive income without working locally. The income requirements, documentation, and application process are entirely separate from the property purchase — and they need to be planned in parallel, not treated as an afterthought once you have completed the purchase.

How to avoid it: If you are planning to live in Spain as a non-EU national, take specialist immigration advice before you commit to a property purchase. Understand the NLV requirements, confirm you meet them, and begin the visa application process well before your planned move date. Coordinate your property purchase and NLV application so that both timelines align.
