Moving to Spain with Dogs: Rules, Paperwork and Practical Tips

No quarantine — but the paperwork must be done in exactly the right order. Here is everything you need to know before you book the flight.

Get Expert Help Back to Blog
Home› Blog› Moving to Spain with Dogs

Moving to Spain on a Non-Lucrative Visa is an exciting step, and for many applicants, the question of how to bring their dog along is just as important as the visa paperwork itself. The good news: there is no quarantine requirement for dogs entering Spain, provided the health documentation is correct and complete. The not-so-good news: the sequence of steps matters enormously, and a single error — such as vaccinating before microchipping, or using the wrong health certificate format — can mean restarting the process with months lost.

This guide covers the rules for dogs entering Spain from non-EU countries including the UK (post-Brexit), the USA, Canada, Australia, South Africa, and New Zealand. It covers the core requirements, country-specific paperwork, airlines, Spanish rules once you are settled, finding vets, and practical tips for helping your dog adjust to their new Mediterranean life.

The Core Requirements: What Every Dog Needs to Enter Spain

Spain is a member of the European Union, and EU rules govern the entry of pet dogs. The three non-negotiable requirements are: a compliant microchip, a current rabies vaccination, and an official health certificate endorsed by your country's government authority. There is no quarantine if these are in order.

1. ISO-Compliant Microchip (First Step — Always)

Your dog must be implanted with a 15-digit ISO 11784/11785 compliant microchip before anything else. This is critical: the microchip must be implanted before or on the same day as the rabies vaccination. If the vaccine is administered before the microchip is confirmed, EU border control will reject the paperwork entirely and your dog will need to be revaccinated — and the 21-day waiting period starts again.

Most UK and US-issued microchips are ISO-compliant, but verify with your vet. Some older US chips (10-digit FDX-A) are not ISO-compliant for EU entry. If in doubt, have your vet scan and confirm the chip number and format is accepted.

2. Rabies Vaccination

After microchipping, your dog must receive a valid rabies vaccination from a licensed veterinarian. If this is a primary (first-ever) rabies vaccination, or if the previous vaccine has lapsed and your dog is being revaccinated as if for the first time, you must wait at least 21 days after vaccination before travelling to Spain. This 21-day wait is mandatory — there are no exceptions.

If your dog has a continuous documented history of rabies vaccination (all boosters given within the valid period of the previous dose), no waiting period is required for boosters. Ensure your vet records clearly show the booster was given within the validity window of the preceding vaccine.

Minimum age: dogs must be at least 15 weeks old to enter Spain — 12 weeks (the earliest age for rabies vaccination) plus the 21-day waiting period.

3. Official EU Health Certificate

For non-EU country travel, dogs must be accompanied by an Official EU Health Certificate completed and endorsed by a government-authorised veterinarian. This certificate must be issued within 10 days of your arrival date in Spain. It is a single-entry document — once you have entered, get a Spanish vet to issue an EU Pet Passport so future travel is simpler.

UK Dogs Post-Brexit: The Animal Health Certificate (AHC)

Brexit changed everything for UK pet owners. As of 1 January 2021, Great Britain (England, Scotland, Wales) is treated as a third country for EU pet travel purposes. The old British Pet Passport is no longer valid for travel to EU countries including Spain.

Instead, UK pet owners must obtain an Animal Health Certificate (AHC) issued by an Official Veterinarian (OV) in the UK. The AHC must be issued no more than 10 days before your arrival in Spain. It is valid for a single entry into the EU.

The medical requirements are the same: ISO microchip, valid rabies vaccination (21-day wait if primary), and the AHC signed by an OV. Spain does not require a tapeworm treatment for dogs arriving from the UK. Your UK OV should use the correct EU format for the AHC — not the old UK-only format.

Once you are settled in Spain, take your dog to a Spanish vet to have an EU Pet Passport issued. The EU Pet Passport is a small blue booklet containing your dog's microchip number, vaccine history, and health records, and it is what you will use for future travel within the EU.

Northern Ireland: Northern Ireland has a different status under the Brexit Withdrawal Agreement. Northern Irish pet owners retain access to EU pet travel procedures and should confirm their specific requirements with their vet.

US Dogs: The USDA APHIS Endorsement Process

For dogs travelling from the United States, the health certificate process involves two stages: preparation by a USDA-accredited veterinarian and physical endorsement by USDA APHIS (Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service).

Spain specifically requires a bilingual (English/Spanish) EU Health Certificate. The standard English-only version will not be accepted at border control. Your USDA-accredited vet must request the correct bilingual Spanish entry template from APHIS well in advance — this template is not standard and must be specifically requested.

Once your vet has completed the certificate using the correct template, the paperwork must be submitted to the relevant APHIS office for physical endorsement. APHIS must physically ink-sign and emboss-stamp the document — a digital or scanned copy is not accepted at the Spanish border. Given APHIS processing times and postal return, plan 6–8 weeks in advance to allow sufficient time, and use a prepaid overnight envelope for the return to ensure timely receipt before your departure.

The endorsed certificate must be issued within 10 days of your arrival in Spain, so coordinate the APHIS endorsement timing carefully with your travel date.

Australia, Canada, New Zealand, South Africa: General Process

Dogs from Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and South Africa follow the same general EU third-country entry process. Each country has its own government authority that must endorse the EU Health Certificate:

  • Australia: The Department of Agriculture, Water and the Environment (DAWE). Request the EU-format certificate from your vet and arrange government endorsement before departure.
  • Canada: The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) endorses export health certificates. A bilingual French/English certificate endorsed by CFIA satisfies EU requirements; confirm with the Spanish consulate whether a Spanish bilingual version is required.
  • New Zealand: The Ministry for Primary Industries (MPI). Follow the EU third-country pet travel requirements and arrange MPI endorsement.
  • South Africa: The Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development (DALRRD). Government endorsement is required.

In all cases, start the process at least 3 months before your travel date to allow for veterinary preparation, government endorsement, and postal processing.

Airlines and Transport Options

Most people moving to Spain with their dog will fly. The rules and costs vary significantly between airlines, so research your options early — especially if your dog is large.

In-Cabin Travel

Small dogs (typically the dog plus carrier combined under 8 kg / approximately 17.6 lbs) can usually travel in the cabin with you, kept in a soft-sided carrier under the seat. Airlines permitting in-cabin pets include Iberia, Vueling, Air Europa, Lufthansa, KLM, and British Airways for qualifying sizes. In-cabin pet fees typically run €50–200 each way depending on the airline and route. Book your pet space at the time of booking your own ticket — airlines cap the number of pets per flight.

Cargo Hold (Excess Baggage)

Larger dogs must travel in the temperature-controlled cargo hold, either as "excess baggage" (on the same flight as you) or as "manifest cargo" (potentially on a different flight). Use an IATA-approved rigid airline crate sized correctly for your dog (the dog must be able to stand, turn, and lie down naturally). Fees for cargo hold travel vary enormously: €150–400 for medium dogs, €400–800+ for large or giant breeds.

Summer embargoes: Many airlines suspend pet cargo during summer months when ground temperatures at some airports exceed safe limits. This can affect travel in June, July, August, and September. Plan around this or choose early morning flights to minimise heat exposure.

Designated entry points: If arriving from a non-EU country, you must enter Spain through a designated Border Inspection Post (BIP) equipped to handle animals. Major international airports (Madrid Barajas, Barcelona El Prat, Malaga, Valencia, Palma, Tenerife Sur) have BIPs. Upon arrival, declare your dog to the border authorities (Guardia Civil or SOIVRE inspectors) who will verify the microchip, health certificate, and documentation.

Spanish Rules for Dog Owners

Once you are settled in Spain, a new set of rules applies to you as a dog owner. Spain has both national and municipal regulations governing dogs, and it is worth familiarising yourself with them early.

Empadronamiento and Animal Registration

Some municipalities require dogs to be registered in the local animal census (censo de animales de compañía or registro municipal de animales) separately from your own empadronamiento. This typically requires presenting your dog's microchip number, vaccination records, and a photo of the dog. There may be a small annual registration fee. Check with your local Ayuntamiento (town hall) about the requirements in your municipality.

Leash Laws and Public Spaces

Throughout Spain, dogs must be kept on a lead in most public spaces — streets, parks, town centres, and on public transport. The lead must typically be no longer than 2 metres. Dogs are generally not permitted on beaches, though many municipalities designate specific beach areas or times (often early morning or off-season) for dogs. Check local municipal ordinances for your specific area.

Potentially Dangerous Breeds (PPP — Perros Potencialmente Peligrosos)

Spain has a national list of breeds classified as Potencialmente Peligrosos (PPP) under Royal Decree 287/2002. Breeds on the list include (but are not limited to): Pit Bull Terrier, Staffordshire Bull Terrier, American Staffordshire Terrier, Rottweiler, Dogo Argentino, Fila Brasileiro, Tosa Inu, and Akita Inu. Mixed-breed dogs that display characteristics of listed breeds may also be classified as PPP by local authorities.

If your dog is or may be classified as PPP, you must:

  • Register for a PPP licence at your local Ayuntamiento (typically within 3 months of arrival or first registering in Spain)
  • Obtain a specific third-party liability insurance policy covering a minimum of €120,000 for damages caused by the dog
  • Keep the dog muzzled in all public spaces
  • Keep the dog on a lead of no more than 2 metres in public
  • Only the licence holder may walk a PPP dog in public

Penalties for non-compliance with PPP rules can be substantial. Some regions and municipalities have their own additional requirements on top of the national framework.

Finding Vets in Spain

Spain has an excellent network of veterinary practices, and in expat-heavy areas (Costa del Sol, Costa Blanca, Alicante, Valencia, Barcelona, Madrid) you will find English-speaking vets without difficulty. Many practices in these areas have multi-lingual staff and are experienced with the international pet documentation requirements.

Finding your vet before you arrive is ideal. Ask other expats in your area through local Facebook groups, community forums, or your NLV application specialist for recommendations. Register your dog with a local vet within the first few weeks of arrival — they will issue your EU Pet Passport, update vaccination records onto the European format, and can advise on any regional health considerations (leishmaniasis prevention is important in many parts of Spain, for example).

Annual costs for basic veterinary care in Spain are generally similar to the UK and somewhat lower than US costs: a routine annual health check and booster vaccinations typically run €80–150. Flea and tick prevention products are available at pharmacies and pet shops as well as veterinary practices; tick prevention is particularly important given the prevalence of tick-borne diseases in rural parts of Spain.

Settling Your Dog into Spanish Life

The move to Spain is an enormous change for your dog — new smells, a new climate, different food and water, new routines. Most dogs adjust well within a few weeks, but some need more time. Here are practical tips for helping your dog settle:

  • Maintain routine: Feed at the same times, walk at the same times where possible. Familiar routines provide comfort during the adjustment period.
  • Gradual temperature acclimatisation: If you are arriving in summer, avoid exercising your dog during the hottest part of the day (typically noon to 5pm). Walk early morning and evening. Pavements can reach temperatures that burn paws on hot days — check with the back of your hand before walking on exposed stone or asphalt.
  • Water: Ensure your dog always has access to fresh water, particularly in warm weather. Spanish tap water is safe in most areas, but some dogs with sensitive stomachs may respond better to bottled water initially while adjusting to different mineral content.
  • Leishmaniasis prevention: This parasitic disease, transmitted by sandflies, is endemic in much of Spain. Speak to your vet about prevention — options include a vaccine (available in Spain), monthly preventative spot-on treatments, and collars (such as Scalibor) that repel sandflies. This is important and something you may not have needed to consider in the UK, US, or Australia.
  • Socialisation: Spain is a dog-loving country and social. Dog-friendly restaurants, bars with terraces, and shops are common. Getting your dog comfortable around Spanish urban environments early makes daily life much easier.
  • ID tag: Even with a microchip, fit your dog with an ID tag showing your Spanish mobile number and address as soon as you have a local SIM. If your dog gets lost in an unfamiliar environment, a tag with a local number gives finders the fastest way to contact you.
