Your Spain Non-Lucrative Visa Was Rejected

You have one month to appeal. Here's what you need to know about why your application was refused, what your options are, and how to move forward.

What Happens After Rejection

When the Spanish consulate refuses an NLV application, you receive a formal refusal letter in Spanish explaining the grounds for the decision. This is a critical moment: the notice tells you which appeal is available and the deadline for it (an administrative appeal is normally one month from notification), or you can address the issues and reapply.

Most rejections are preventable — they stem from correctable errors like wrong health insurance, missing apostilles, or incomplete income documentation. The good news is that understanding the specific reason for your refusal puts you in control of the next step.

Asked for More Documents, or Refused? How to Tell

A request for documents is not a refusal

If the consulate or immigration office writes asking for a missing or corrected document (a requerimiento), the application is still open. The request states how long you have to reply — follow it. Under the general procedure law the period is ten days; an extension of up to five days can be granted when documents are hard to obtain, but it is not automatic, so ask before the deadline if you need it. If you don't reply in time, the application can be treated as withdrawn (Ley 39/2015, art. 68). See what to do if the consulate asks for more documents.

A refusal is a formal decision with reasons

A refusal letter sets out the grounds and, at the end, the appeals available and their deadlines — consular and immigration-office decisions do not all follow the same route, so the notice is what counts. Typically that is an administrative appeal within one month (for example, asking the same authority to reconsider, a recurso de reposición: Ley 39/2015, art. 124) and/or a court appeal (recurso contencioso-administrativo) within two months (Ley 29/1998, art. 46). Or you can fix the problem and reapply. See appeal or reapply: how to decide.

If health insurance was the reason

Replace the policy with one that meets the requirement and get the certificate your consulate expects, then reapply or appeal. You can buy your health insurance through us whoever handles your visa — see rejected health insurance: what to do.

Your Appeal Window — Follow the Deadline in Your Notice

If you decide to appeal, timing is critical. Your refusal notice states which appeal applies, where to lodge it and the deadline. Under Spain's general administrative procedure, an administrative appeal must be lodged within one month of notification, counted date to date (Ley 39/2015, arts. 30.4, 122 and 124) — not 30 days.

Appeal Timeline at a Glance

  • Within the deadline in your notice: Submit formal appeal with supporting documentation
  • Week 1-8: Consulate reviews your appeal
  • Month 2-3: Decision issued
Detailed Timeline

Appeal success depends on: The specific grounds for refusal, strength of your counter-evidence, and how well you address each point in the refusal letter. Many applicants don't understand that an appeal isn't just asking them to reconsider — it requires new or additional evidence that addresses the original rejection reasons.

Understanding the Appeal Process

The Spanish administrative appeal process (recurso de alzada) follows a formal structure. Your appeal must:

  1. Reference the specific refusal letter and date
  2. Address each ground for rejection point-by-point
  3. Provide new evidence or clarify misunderstandings
  4. Be submitted in Spanish
  5. Meet the one-month deadline

Appeal Success Rates & Real Scenarios

Appeals succeed when you can prove the original rejection was based on a misunderstanding or when you provide documentation that was missing or unclear the first time. The most successful appeals address:

  • Health insurance issues (if you now have compliant cover)
  • Document errors (missing apostilles corrected, new translations provided)
  • Income documentation (additional bank statements, clarity on source)
  • Misinterpretation of regulations
